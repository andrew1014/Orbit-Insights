🚀 The Rocket That Keeps Getting Told No

📅 September 4, 2026 📂 European Space Tech

This morning, a 28-meter rocket built by a Munich startup got told no for the seventh time this year. Same week, SpaceX flew its 104th orbital mission of 2026. The gap between those two facts is the whole story.

This Morning, Above the Arctic Circle

Around 10:39 this morning, local time, Norwegian police sealed the roads and the sea lanes around a spaceport on an island 200 kilometers north of the Arctic Circle. Just before 2:30, they quietly reopened them. In between, the launch community pieced together what happened from police bulletins and weather data: about 24 knots of wind — too much for a 28-meter rocket balancing on nine engines.

Scrubbed. Again. The seventh time since January.

And this time, the company didn't say a word. No statement, no tweet, no update. For a team that has posted a public explanation after every single scrub — valve problems, a leak, a fishing boat — the silence was louder than any of them.

The window isn't closed, though. Norwegian authorities cleared launch operations from September 4 through 10, with the shot opening at 4 p.m. Eastern each evening and staying open for an hour. If you're reading this Friday night, the next attempt is expected Saturday at 4 p.m. ET.

Last week, we watched the most democratic telescope ever built ride a Falcon Heavy to open the sky to everyone. This week is the other end of that same story: a rocket that just needs one yes.

Here's the thesis: while the machine-cadence incumbents fly their 104th mission of the year without breaking a sweat, the future of European access to space is riding on a rocket that has been told no seven times. Because the future isn't built by whoever has the biggest budget. It's built by whoever keeps showing up.

A Rocket That Has Never Done the Hard Part

The mission is called "Onward and Upward." It's the qualification flight for Isar Aerospace's Spectrum rocket — the second flight of the vehicle, and the first one carrying passengers.

Here's what Spectrum has to do that it has never done in flight, in order: shut down its first stage; separate the stages; ignite its second-stage engine in vacuum; jettison the payload fairing; restart that engine to circularize the orbit; then deploy its satellites one by one.

Every successful orbital rocket ever built has one thing in common: it had to do each of those things for the first time, at altitude, in public. Stage separation is where rockets go to die. Spectrum has never gotten that far.

Its only flight so far lasted about 30 seconds. On March 30, 2025, Spectrum became the first orbital launch attempt from mainland Europe — outside of the pads Russia inherited — when it lifted off from Andøya and then received a termination order seconds later, falling into the Norwegian Sea. The New York Times ran the headline "Orbital Rocket Crashes After First Launch From Continental Europe."

The team's response to losing their rocket in the ocean? "We launch, learn, and repeat." That is the entire philosophy of this company in five words.

Seven Nos, and What They Actually Mean

The campaign since January reads like a comedy of spaceflight errors, except nobody's laughing:

  • January 21 — scrubbed on a faulty pressurization valve.
  • March 25 — got to T-minus 3 seconds, when an unauthorized fishing vessel drifted into the danger zone. By the time the range was cleared, the liquid propane in the tanks had warmed out of spec. Propane is picky; a hold on a warm day ruins the fuel.
  • April 9 — a suspected leak in a composite overwrapped pressure vessel, the high-pressure gas bottle that drives the whole propulsion system.
  • June 15 and 18 — off-nominal behavior in the vehicle's fluid systems. Twice.
  • Today — wind.

The pattern isn't bad luck, and it isn't incompetence. It's diagnostic. The recurring failures are all in pressurization and fluid systems — the exact subsystems you have to invent yourself when you build a rocket nearly entirely in-house, the way Isar does: its own engines, its own pressure vessels, its own plumbing.

SpaceX went through the same kind of pain with COPVs on Falcon 9 back in 2016. Every anomaly here is a bespoke problem this team is solving for the first time, because there's no supplier history to lean on. That's the cost of the vertical-integration bet. It's also why the scrubs are proof of progress, not proof of failure.

The Manifest Is Homework

Here's the part that keeps making me smile: the first paying passengers on this rocket are, mostly, students.

The five CubeSats and one experiment were chosen through the German space agency DLR's Microlauncher competition — which Isar won back in April 2021 — with support from ESA's Boost! programme:

  • CyBEEsat (TU Berlin) — a cybersecurity radio experiment plus radiation-tolerant perovskite solar cells.
  • SpaceTeamSat1 (TU Wien Space Team) — student-written software running in orbit.
  • FramSat-1 (NTNU, Norway) — a Norwegian student satellite; ESA's director general specifically said he was proud this mission carries it.
  • TriSat-S (University of Maribor, Slovenia) — tests battery behavior after hibernation and a new optical navigation system.
  • Platform 6 (EnduroSat, Bulgaria) — commercial rideshare hardware.
  • Let It Go (Dcubed, Germany) — a non-separable experiment that stays on the upper stage, testing solar-sail tech for dragging dead spacecraft out of orbit.

A rocket that has never reached orbit is carrying homework. Five student-built or student-operated satellites from four countries, integrated by Berlin's Exolaunch, heading for a sun-synchronous orbit.

If this works, the first successful orbital launch from continental European soil will have carried a university cybersecurity demo, a Norwegian student satellite, and a solar sail to space. That's not a footnote to the story. That's the story.

Liquid Propane and Vertical Integration

The rocket itself is a weird, clever little machine. Spectrum stands 28 meters tall, 2 meters wide, about 60 tons on the pad. Ten engines total: nine Aquila sea-level engines on the first stage (75 kilonewtons each, 675 combined) and one vacuum-optimized Aquila VAC on the second.

It burns liquid oxygen and liquid propane. Not kerosene, not methane — propane. The choice is pure engineering economics: propane packs more energy per liter than RP-1, so the tanks can be smaller and the whole vehicle stays compact. Compact means cheaper to build, cheaper to fly, cheaper to scale. From Andøya it can lift about 700 kilograms to sun-synchronous orbit; from a planned second pad in French Guiana, 1,000 kilograms to low Earth orbit.

Isar has run more than 124 hotfire tests on Aquila, including one engine fired six consecutive times without a rebuild. But ground testing doesn't replicate the vacuum ignition after stage separation. None of these engines has done the hardest version of its job yet.

The money side says the market believes in the approach: more than half a billion dollars raised, including a €270 million Series D announced this year, and a reported €197.8 million from ESA's European Launcher Challenge — a contract that is explicitly structured around reaching orbit by the end of 2027.

Three Clocks Are Ticking

The contracts add up to real pressure. TechTimes laid it out this week: the ESA commitment with its 2027 orbital deadline; a $112.5 million deal to build a dedicated Spectrum pad in Nova Scotia, with a handover date of November 1; and a 12-month delivery clock for Planet Labs Germany that started July 2. Three separate obligations, all riding on a vehicle that has never completed stage separation.

And underneath all of it is the geopolitical reason this matters: Europe has no orbital launch pad on its own soil. Every European orbital launch since the space age began has left from Kourou in French Guiana — which is South America. When Russia invaded Ukraine, Europe lost Soyuz. Ariane 6 ran years late. Vega-C had failures. A continent that built the modern world has been hitching rides to its own future.

If Spectrum works, it isn't just a startup milestone. It's the first time a rocket designed, built, and launched from western Europe puts something into orbit. Ever.

The Real Contrast

This is the week to feel the gap. SpaceX's 104th orbital launch of 2026 happened Tuesday — a Falcon 9 from California, 27 Starlinks, routine, done. The company is on track for its 700th Falcon 9 mission this month. They've automated the sky so completely that a launch is background noise.

And on the same planet, in the same week, a Munich startup with a rocket the size of a utility pole got told no by the wind.

I think both of those are the future. The machine that never misses — and the underdog that won't stop showing up. Every subculture that ever changed the mainstream did it the same way: not by out-shouting the big players, but by out-lasting them. The underground doesn't win by being louder. It wins by being there on the day everyone else gave up.

Seven nos. The team is still fueling.

The Honest Part

I'm not going to oversell this. It might fail. Stage separation is where rockets die, and most first qualification flights of this commercial era end in a fireball or a splash. The odds are genuinely not in Spectrum's favor.

But even the attempt changes the math. A company founded in 2018 — from a Munich office to a pad above the Arctic Circle, designing, building, and launching almost everything in-house — is the same pattern I keep seeing everywhere I look for the future: people at the edges building the infrastructure the mainstream won't build, because they're the ones who actually need it.

And if it flies? The first successful orbital launch from continental Europe will have carried homework to space. I can't get over that detail, and I don't want to.

The Question I Keep Coming Back To

Last week we asked who gets to see the universe when the sky opens up. This week the question is simpler: who gets to build the door?

The window stays open through September 10. The next shot is Saturday, 4 p.m. Eastern. I'll be watching from my desk, the same way I've watched every attempt since the first one fell into the Norwegian Sea.
