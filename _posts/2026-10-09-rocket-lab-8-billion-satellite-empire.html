🚀 NASA and Boeing Rejected Him. Now He's Buying a Satellite Empire.

📅 October 9, 2026 📂 Rocket Lab & Vertical Integration

The loudest story in space this year was a rocket finally reaching orbit. The quietest — and probably the more important one — is a company that started in a garage in New Zealand and just agreed to spend $8 billion to own the whole stack. It's also, this month, fighting NASA in Washington over a contract it didn't win.

The short version

  • What: Rocket Lab (Nasdaq: RKLB) agreed on June 29, 2026 to acquire Iridium Communications for about $8.0 billion — $54 a share, half cash, half Rocket Lab stock.
  • Approved: Iridium's shareholders voted it through on September 24, 2026, with 99.6% in favor. Closing is expected in mid-2027, pending regulators.
  • What it buys: Iridium's 66-satellite, cross-linked low-Earth-orbit network, globally coordinated L-band spectrum, 2.55 million subscribers, and 500+ partner companies.
  • The size gap: Iridium made $871.7M in revenue and $114.4M in net income in 2025. Rocket Lab made $601.8M and lost $198.2M.
  • The fight: On September 11, 2026, Rocket Lab filed a GAO bid protest over NASA's ~$700 million Mars Telecommunications Network award to Blue Origin. NASA suspended the contract; a ruling is expected by mid-December.
  • The rockets: Electron has flown 75+ missions. Neutron — 43 meters, reusable, 13,000 kg to low Earth orbit — targets a first flight in late 2026.
Here's the thesis: the most important thing happening in space right now isn't a launch. It's a business model. For years, exactly one Western company owned its rockets, built its satellites, and ran its own network — SpaceX. That vertical integration is the moat, not the engines. Rocket Lab just agreed to spend $8 billion to buy the second copy of it, while fighting NASA over a Mars contract it thinks it should have won. The underdog isn't trying to be louder. It's trying to own the whole stack. That's the quietest, most ambitious bet in the industry — and almost nobody's watching it.

Two stories in space, and only one gets the camera

When Starship reached orbit last month, it earned a thousand headlines, and I added to them. But there are two stories running in commercial space at the same time, and only one of them is on camera.

The loud one is the spectacle: the biggest rocket ever built, the heat shield, the catch. The quiet one is a business model. And the quiet one is what decides which companies are still standing in ten years.

Here's the distinction that matters. Anyone can build a rocket. Anyone can build a satellite. Almost nobody can do both, own the network, and run the whole thing as one machine. In the West, exactly one company has pulled that off at scale: SpaceX. Falcon launches Starlink, Starlink sells the bandwidth, and the money funds the next rocket. It's a closed loop, and it's why SpaceX's lead compounds instead of shrinking.

This week's story is that someone is trying to build a second copy of that loop.

What $8 billion actually buys

On June 29, Rocket Lab agreed to buy Iridium Communications for about $8 billion — $54 a share, split between cash and Rocket Lab stock. On September 24, Iridium's shareholders approved it, 99.6% in favor. The deal should close in mid-2027, once regulators weigh in.

Look at the size gap and the whole strategy jumps out. In 2025, Iridium made $871.7 million in revenue and $114.4 million in net income. Rocket Lab made $601.8 million and lost $198.2 million. A smaller, unprofitable company is buying a larger, profitable one.

That's not a typo. It's the point. Rocket Lab isn't buying Iridium for its satellites — those are 66 spacecraft launched between 2017 and 2019, and they'll need replacing. It's buying Iridium for two things you can't manufacture: a paying customer base, and spectrum.

Why spectrum, not rockets, is the real prize

Spectrum is the boring word that decides everything in satellite communications. Iridium holds globally coordinated L-band frequencies — radio real estate that took years of international negotiation to assemble, and almost never comes up for sale.

Rocket Lab's CEO, Peter Beck, had told investors the hard parts of running a constellation weren't the rockets or the satellites. They were securing harmonized spectrum and a base of paying customers. Buying Iridium is the shortcut around both at once. It also opens doors Rocket Lab couldn't build alone: direct-to-device service that links ordinary phones straight to satellites, and positioning and timing signals that can back up GPS when it's jammed. That last one is what defense and aviation customers actually pay for.

The garage kid

Here's the human part, and honestly it's the reason I care about this at all.

Peter Beck grew up in Invercargill, at the bottom of New Zealand's South Island — a town better known for sheep than spacecraft. As a teenager he turbocharged the family Mini and launched water rockets in the garage. He never went to university. He started out as a tool-and-die apprentice at an appliance company, and by his own account he got turned down, more than once, when he applied to NASA and Boeing — because he didn't have an engineering degree.

So he built his own company instead. Rocket Lab was founded in 2006, when Beck was 29. Before the orbital rockets worked, he was building rocket bikes and jet packs in a workshop, because nobody would let him build the real thing.

I keep coming back to that. The space industry loves to talk about the future being built by visionaries with capital. Beck started with neither. He had a workshop, and a refusal to accept that the door was closed.

The fight in Washington

As if buying a phone network weren't enough, Rocket Lab is also picking a fight.

In May 2026, NASA asked industry to propose a Mars Telecommunications Network — a relay satellite that would let Mars surface missions send data home at high bandwidth. NASA picked Blue Origin for the job: an estimated $700 million to build, launch, and operate a single Mars orbiter by the end of 2028.

On September 11, Rocket Lab formally protested that decision with the U.S. Government Accountability Office, arguing NASA's evaluation ignored eligibility rules Congress wrote for commercial deep-space infrastructure. The protest triggered an automatic freeze on the contract; NASA has since said it suspended Blue Origin's award while the GAO reviews. A decision is expected by mid-December 2026.

Why does that deadline matter? Because Mars only lines up with Earth once every 26 months. Miss the 2028 window and the whole thing slips toward 2030.

Blue Origin gets a taste of its own medicine

There's a detail here that's almost too tidy.

Back in 2021, Blue Origin lost a major NASA contract — the first crewed lunar lander — to SpaceX, and did exactly what Rocket Lab just did: it formally protested the award to the GAO. Plenty of people called it sour grapes from a company that had simply been out-competed.

Now the positions have flipped. Blue Origin is the incumbent, and the scrappy challenger crying foul is Rocket Lab. The Register put it plainly: Blue Origin is getting a taste of its own medicine.

I'm not going to pretend that's justice, or that Rocket Lab's protest is obviously right — I can't see the bids, and neither can you. What the reversal does reveal is simpler: in commercial space, nobody stays the underdog, and nobody stays the giant. The industry turns over faster than the reputations do.

The story nobody's watching

SpaceX is a machine for producing headlines. Rocket Lab is quietly turning into a company that does everything.

Electron has flown more than 75 missions and is the second-most-launched American orbital rocket after Falcon 9. In June, Rocket Lab did something no one else had: it launched an Electron just 16 hours and 42 minutes after the Space Force gave the formal go — beating the previous responsive-launch record of 27 hours — with a satellite it had also built itself and now operates. Design, build, launch, operate, all under one roof. That's the vertical-integration thesis proven in miniature.

The next piece is Neutron, a 43-meter reusable medium-lift rocket — 13,000 kilograms to low Earth orbit, squarely Falcon 9's territory. It's been delayed repeatedly and now targets a first flight in late 2026. Beck says he won't rush it: "You won't see us minimising some qualifier about us just clearing the pad and claiming success."

Here's the subculture thread I keep pulling in this newsletter: the future gets made on the margins, not in the center. Beck is a margin guy — no degree, no connections, a workshop in a town nobody was watching. And the culture he came out of even has a name for what happens to people who make it big anyway: tall poppy syndrome, the New Zealand habit of cutting down anyone who stands too tall. Beck has said he's felt it himself.

That's the same tension running through every subculture I write about. Build something real on the edges, and the center will either ignore you or try to knock you down. The ones who last do it anyway, and let the work speak.

The honest part

None of this is guaranteed, and I'd be a bad guide if I pretended otherwise.

The deal doesn't close until mid-2027, and regulators still have to approve the transfer of globally coordinated spectrum and a defense-adjacent satellite network — the kind of thing that draws real scrutiny. Comments are due to the FCC on October 15.

Rocket Lab is still losing money, and it's now swallowing a much bigger operation without disrupting the subscribers and government contracts that make Iridium valuable. The satellites need replacing on a clock, and Neutron — the rocket meant to replenish them cheaply — keeps slipping. If Neutron slides badly, the whole rationale for owning the constellation weakens.

And Rocket Lab isn't alone. In April, Amazon agreed to buy Iridium's old rival Globalstar for about $11.6 billion, to power direct-to-device features. Two of the three legacy mobile-satellite operators got absorbed within roughly ten weeks. The window for buying a network like Iridium may already be closing.

So this is a bet, not a coronation. An $8 billion bet, from a company that started in a garage.

One last thing

The thing I keep thinking about is how quietly the real moves happen.

Starship reaching orbit was on every front page, and it earned that. But the story that decides what the space industry looks like in 2035 isn't a launch. It's whether a scrappy company from the bottom of New Zealand can buy the one asset SpaceX spent a decade building by hand — and whether the people who write the rules will let him.

Beck watches launches from his office, not mission control, and has said they're "not for fun." That's the part the headlines never capture: the people building the future are usually the ones who didn't get let in the front door.
