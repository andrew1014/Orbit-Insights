🚀 Seven Nos, Then a Yes

📅 September 12, 2026 📂 European Space Tech

Last week I wrote about a rocket that had been told no seven times. On Saturday night, at 10:12 pm local time above the Arctic Circle, it got a yes. Seven minutes later, it was in orbit.

What Actually Happened on Saturday Night

At 20:12 UTC on September 5, Isar Aerospace's Spectrum rocket lifted off from Andøya Spaceport in northern Norway — 200 kilometers above the Arctic Circle — turned north over the Norwegian Sea, and reached orbit seven minutes later.

Here's what had to go right, in order, and this is the part the highlight reels skip. Nine Aquila engines burned propane and liquid oxygen for two and a half minutes. The vehicle pushed through MaxQ — the moment aerodynamic pressure peaks and the airframe takes its worst beating. Main engine cutoff. Stage separation, the maneuver every rocket engineer will tell you is where rockets go to die. Second-stage ignition in vacuum. Through the Kármán line at 100 kilometers, the legal edge of space. Payload fairing jettisoned. Then roughly 7.8 kilometers per second — about five miles per second — fast enough that the planet stops pulling you back.

Then a coast halfway around the world, a relight of the second stage to circularize the orbit, and one by one, the payloads.

All five CubeSats separated. Isar is working with the teams now to confirm each satellite is alive and talking — its own slow, unglamorous process that takes days, not minutes.

And because the launch happened at dusk, sunlight caught the second stage's exhaust plume and turned it into a jellyfish: the blue-white spiral that shows up over the Arctic a few times a year and makes everyone's phone camera look better than it is. People who have never opened a space website saw that on Saturday. That matters more than any press release.

The Seven Nos, One Line Each

A faulty pressurization valve in January. A fishing boat in the exclusion zone in March, which warmed the liquid propane out of spec while the range was cleared. A suspected leak in a composite overwrapped pressure vessel in April. Off-nominal behavior in the fluid systems in June — twice. Wind in early September. A hold on September 4 that pushed the shot to the weekend.

And underneath all of it, the older wound: the first Spectrum flight, March 30, 2025, died 30 seconds after liftoff. A vent valve opened when it shouldn't have, the rocket lost attitude control during its pitch-over, and the range sent the termination command at T+30 seconds. It fell into the sea near its own pad.

Two flights. One success. And every single failure landed in the same boring category: pressurization and fluid systems. The plumbing. Nobody writes a headline about a valve seat.

That's not bad luck, and it isn't incompetence. It's the cost of a deliberate bet. Isar builds its own engines, its own pressure vessels, its own plumbing — a vertical integration play that means every problem is one no supplier has already solved for them. It also means the fixes belong to them, permanently. SpaceX hit the same class of problem with Falcon 9 COPVs in 2016 and solved it the same way: by owning the whole stack.

Here's the thesis: when I wrote about this rocket last week, I said the future gets built by whoever keeps showing up. Saturday, that stopped being a nice line and became a flight record. Orbit isn't a prize for the smartest team in the room. It's what's left over after everyone else quits the plumbing.

The Team That Kept Fueling

Daniel Metzler, Josef Fleischmann, and Markus Brandl met at the Technical University of Munich. In 2018 they started tinkering with rocket parts in a campus workshop, and eventually decided they might be able to make a business out of the hobby. They named the company after the river that runs through Munich.

Metzler, speaking to reporters the day after the launch:

"Especially early on, back in 2018, when, frankly, there was no 'New Space' industry, especially on this side of the Atlantic, people said it will never work. People will continue to always say this will not work, and that will not work, until we just prove them wrong. Giving up is not in our DNA."

And this one, which is the actual reason I keep writing about this stuff instead of just tracking stock prices:

"Don't forget the human element behind all of this. There are engineers, technicians, operators, a strong support team, who just dare to dream, and against all odds, that team delivered."

Eight years. Roughly $1 billion raised privately, on a continent with far less venture capital for space than the US. Nearly 500 employees. And here's the detail that reframes the whole thing: the rocket was not developed under a government contract. The German space agency DLR put in 11 million euros ($13 million) back in 2021 — mainly to arrange the ride for the student CubeSats on this flight. The vehicle itself was private money, built to sell launches on a global market.

"The Spectrum rocket was not developed under a government contract," said Walther Pelzer, director general of DLR. "It was developed to serve a global market."

The Manifest Was Homework

Qualification flights are usually a mass simulator: a block of metal that weighs the right amount and proves nothing except that the math worked. Spectrum's fairing held five small satellites and one experiment, chosen through DLR's Microlauncher Competition, funded through ESA's Boost! programme:

  • CyBEEsat (TU Berlin) — a cybersecurity radio experiment plus radiation-tolerant perovskite solar cells.
  • SpaceTeamSat1 (TU Wien Space Team) — student-written software, in orbit.
  • FramSat-1 (NTNU, Norway) — a Norwegian student satellite.
  • TriSat-S (University of Maribor, Slovenia) — battery behavior after hibernation, plus an optical navigation experiment.
  • Platform 6 (EnduroSat, Bulgaria) — commercial rideshare hardware.
  • Let It Go (Dcubed, Germany) — a non-separating experiment that stays attached to the upper stage, testing solar-sail hardware for dragging dead spacecraft out of orbit.

So the first fully commercial European rocket to reach orbit carried a university cybersecurity demo, a Norwegian student satellite, and a sail for cleaning up orbit. Homework, and a broom. I can't get over that, and I don't want to.

The Number That Actually Matters

It was the second flight of this vehicle. Not the twentieth. Orbit on attempt two.

Put that next to the record: Rocket Lab's Electron reached orbit on its second attempt. Firefly's Alpha needed three. SpaceX's Falcon 1 needed four. Nobody remembers the rockets that quit after the second try, because there are no stories about them.

Which doesn't make Isar the next Rocket Lab. It makes them the next company that gets to find out. And the thing that separates the launch companies that make it from the ones that don't is almost never the first flight. It's the willingness to file, dissect, and fix the same unglamorous failure mode over and over until it stops showing up.

What It Actually Changes for Europe

Europe's problem was never engineering. It's that every European orbital launch in history has left from Kourou — in French Guiana. South America. The continent's flagship rocket, Ariane 6, works, but it's expendable, expensive, and flies about ten times a year. European governments have spent decades either paying a premium to launch on their own rockets or swallowing the politics and buying a ride on a Falcon 9.

Now a private company with no geographical-return strings can put about 700 kilograms into a sun-synchronous orbit from Norwegian soil — more than Rocket Lab's Electron, in the same class as Firefly's Alpha, less than Italy's Vega-C. From a second pad planned in Kourou, that number goes up to a ton to low Earth orbit. That's the actual news: not "Europe launched a rocket," but "European satellite operators now have a launch option that isn't a government program."

The money says the same thing. On August 27, ESA awarded Isar 197.8 million euros ($230 million) under its European Launcher Challenge — the largest of three contracts handed out — with a requirement to reach orbit no later than 2027. Isar cleared that requirement eight days later. Rocket Factory Augsburg in Germany and PLD Space in Spain are both aiming for first orbital attempts by the end of the year; MaiaSpace, the ArianeGroup subsidiary, is negotiating to become the fourth.

Chancellor Friedrich Merz congratulated the team and called it the "beginning of a new era." ESA's director general, Josef Aschbacher, put it cleaner: "Spectrum quite literally rose to the challenge and delivered its payloads in low-Earth orbit."

The Heavier Detail

Spectrum is now the largest rocket ever built in Germany. The previous record-holder was the V-2 — the World War II weapon whose engineers, led by Wernher von Braun, were brought to the United States after the war to build the rockets that eventually took people to the Moon.

I keep sitting with that. The biggest German rocket in history used to mean a terror weapon pointed at cities. The new one carried a Slovenian battery experiment, a Norwegian student satellite, and a sail designed to clean up dead satellites. Same country, eighty years apart.

You don't get to choose your country's history. You do get to choose what you build next. There's something in that worth more than a launch record.

The Honest Part

One success is not a business. Isar says vehicles 3 through 7 are already in production, and a new 40,000-square-meter factory near Munich is meant to eventually support up to 40 rockets a year. Metzler's own timeline for that: "Realistically, it's going to take a few years until we will actually be there."

Also worth saying plainly: Spectrum is expendable. There's no booster landing here, no reuse story. The bet is that in the small and medium satellite market, a rocket that exists and flies is worth more right now than a rocket that's theoretically cheaper later.

And the two things Metzler said that I've been thinking about all week. First: "You cannot simulate your way into orbit." Second, on why scaling is harder than launching: "Our margins of error are so small when you compare it to other industries. Sometimes, even a tolerance of a few micrometers on a single part out of 100,000 parts can make the difference between a payload that gets separated" — and one that doesn't.

Why This Is the Same Story I Keep Finding

Last week the question was who gets to build the door. We got an answer, and it wasn't a government, and it wasn't a billionaire. It was three guys who met at a German university, and a few hundred engineers who spent eight years learning to make valves behave.

This is the pattern I keep finding at the edges of this industry, and it looks exactly like the pattern I find in subcultures. The student CubeSat teams. The workshop that starts in a campus basement because nobody handed them a program. The community that builds the thing it needs because the mainstream looked past it. None of them wait for permission or a funding line. They ship.

Underground scenes work the same way. The ones that change the mainstream are never the loudest — they're the ones still building when everyone else's attention moved on. Isar was still building. So were the four university teams whose satellites rode on top of it.

And the next European company that wants to do this has a much shorter list of unknowns now, because someone went first in public, with the failures on the record. That's the part that compounds.

One Last Thing

Seven nos, then a yes. On the ground they found a vent valve, a boat, a pressure vessel, some fluid lines, and a lot of wind.

Which means the honest version of this story isn't "a startup beat the odds." It's that the odds were never the point. The point was doing the boring part well enough, for long enough, that luck finally had somewhere to land.
