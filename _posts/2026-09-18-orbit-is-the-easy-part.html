🚀 Orbit Is the Easy Part

📅 September 18, 2026 📂 Starship & Reuse

On Monday, if the weather and the FAA cooperate, Starship will finally go to orbit and stay there. The detail I can't stop thinking about is smaller than a dinner plate: two ceramic tiles, pried off a rocket that spent 24 days floating in the Indian Ocean, are scheduled to fly again.

The short version

  • What: Flight 14 — the first Starship mission that attempts to reach orbit rather than arc up and fall back down.
  • When: Monday, September 28. A 75-minute window from 7:15 a.m. Central, pending FAA approval.
  • Ship 41: lights its vacuum Raptors, reaches about 7.8 km/s, settles into a ~275 km orbit, coasts six laps over roughly 10 hours, then deorbits and splashes down in the Pacific west of Chile.
  • Booster 21: no tower catch this time. Splashdown in the Gulf about seven minutes in.
  • Payload: 26 Starlink V3 satellites, about 2,000 kg each. On Flight 13 they deployed on a suborbital arc and burned up 20 minutes later. This time they stay.
Here's the thesis: orbit is a checkbox. The belly is the product. Thirteen flights since April 2023 have all flown what SpaceX calls a "passively safe suborbital trajectory" — a rehearsal that skipped the hardest scene. Going up is math and engines. Coming back at Mach 25 is a materials problem, and materials problems don't care how good your software is.

What came back from Flight 13

Flight 13, on July 24, was the good one. The ship survived reentry, flipped, splashed down in the Indian Ocean and — a first — stayed intact after tipping over. Recovery crews towed it toward Christmas Island, engineers took heat shield samples, and on August 26 it was loaded onto a heavy-lift ship for the ride back to Texas.

That gave the program the thing it had never had: a flown heat shield, on the ground, up close.

The drone footage showed only a few tiles missing, which is genuinely good news. It also showed white streaks across the shield, every one of them starting at the boundary between tiles, plus cracked tiles and broken edges. Read that plainly: hot gas found the gaps.

Dan Rasky, who spent nearly four decades on heat shield materials at NASA Ames and co-invented the material on Crew Dragon's shield, told Ars Technica: "Starship's current thermal protection system is a dead-end for all missions that require full and rapid reusability."

Why the Shuttle already proved tiles can work — and still failed

Rasky and two other former NASA hands — astronaut Charles Camarda and Charles Miller, who ran NASA's transition team — published an analysis arguing SpaceX's approach is a real improvement on the Shuttle's tiles, but built on the same fundamental idea. The Shuttle's tiles worked. What never worked was the turnaround: every tile needed inspection, and inspection is what killed rapid reuse.

What SpaceX is actually trying

To be fair to SpaceX, they aren't pretending otherwise. Musk has called a reusable heat shield "the biggest problem that remains" for Starship, and said the company can't get where it's going with laborious inspection of the whole shield between flights. They've added retention hardware to the highest-risk tiles, opened flow paths that let plasma sneak behind tiles rather than force its way through, and are testing a curved tile design that reduces heating in the gaps.

Two tiles, out of eighteen thousand

Two tiles recovered from Ship 40 will fly again on Ship 41. It's the first time SpaceX has reused a heat shield tile on Starship. Two. Out of roughly eighteen thousand.

I love this, and not as a joke about how small it is. Reusability doesn't start as a policy or a press release. It starts as a number you can count on one hand. The first Falcon 9 booster to fly twice was a stunt. The first tile to fly twice is the same kind of stunt, and it's the only kind that ever turns into an industry.

The people who collect the tiles (yes, really)

For about two years, the people tracking Starship's heat shield most obsessively weren't engineers. They were enthusiasts comparing tile-loss photos flight over flight, arguing about gap fillers in forum threads, and walking the beaches to collect the tiles that fell off.

The tiles float. That's the whole reason the hobby exists. After the first integrated flight in April 2023, Joe Tegtmeyer found half a tile on South Padre Island, about seven miles from the pad, hours after launch — he geolocated it, told SpaceX, and they thanked him and let him keep it. A 2024 profile in the San Antonio Express-News followed Ron Parker, who made eleven trips driving Mexican beaches, found about 120 intact tiles and hundreds of fragments, and sold the best ones on eBay, where listings have run from under a dollar for a shard to several hundred for a whole one. SpaceX runs a debris hotline for exactly this, and under the Outer Space Treaty the debris is still SpaceX's property even after it washes up on a beach.

Easy to file under "space nerds being space nerds." I think it's the opposite. People fixate on the part that reveals the real constraint. Nobody collects launch pad concrete. They collect the tiles, because the tiles are where the hard problem lives — and because a tile is the only piece of a rocket you can hold in your hand. Now the mission has three camera-equipped satellites pointed at them. The obsession became a requirement.

What to watch on Monday

  • Did the orbital insertion burn happen at all? The whole flight plan hinges on the ship being healthy enough after separation for the team to commit to it. Watch for confirmation of the burn, not just that the ship is in space.
  • Do the satellites reach their orbit? Deployment is one thing; 26 satellites climbing to 275 km on their own thrusters is another. That's what turns a test flight into revenue.
  • And weeks from now, the tiles. Not the streaks — the boundaries. If the gaps hold, that's the real headline, and it won't be the one on Monday.

One more: the booster. On Flight 13, three of Booster 20's centre engines clogged with ice during the landing burn, leaving only 8 of 13 lit and turning the splashdown into a hard one. Booster 21 has new filtration hardware and relight software. Land it soft, and a booster catch follows on the next flight.

The honest part

Reaching orbit is a real threshold and I'm not going to pretend otherwise. It's also the first Starship flight expected to make money — SpaceX's CFO told investors this month that Flight 14 will be revenue-generating, because those 26 satellites go to work instead of burning up. After three years of flights that were pure cost, that's a different kind of first.

But three hard things still sit between this flight and the future SpaceX keeps describing: catching the ship, refuelling it in orbit, and a heat shield that survives without an inspection crew. NASA's own estimate for the Artemis lander is ten to sixteen tanker flights to refuel a single ship in orbit, and a GAO report in July found SpaceX more than a year behind on key lander milestones. None of that is a reason to be cynical. It's the reason to discount the schedule talk you hear from everyone else.

So: Monday's flight is worth watching, and it is not the finish line. It's the first time the ship has to come home from somewhere it can't drift back from. That's a new problem, and new problems are the interesting kind.

One last thing

A heat shield is the only part of a rocket designed to be destroyed slowly, and the only part whose whole job is to make the next flight possible. No camera time. No applause. If it works perfectly, you'll never hear its name.

Which is true of most of the things that hold our lives together. The plumbing. The pressure vessel. The valve seat. The tile.
